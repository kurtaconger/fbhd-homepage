Primary Care Practitioners in the Research Triangle

What you're actually choosing when you pick a "PCP" on your health plan

When you select an ACA Marketplace plan, COBRA continuation, or a private policy, the network directory will list your primary care options under one umbrella — "Primary Care Provider" — but the credentials behind that label vary widely. The differences matter for both your out-of-pocket cost and the depth of clinical judgment you're paying for.


The federal Affordable Care Act (Section 5501) recognizes four credential types as "primary care practitioners": physicians, physician assistants, nurse practitioners, and clinical nurse specialists, practicing in family medicine, internal medicine, pediatric medicine, or geriatric medicine.1 Most commercial insurers and the BlueCross BlueShield of NC marketplace plans use this same four-credential framework when designating in-network PCPs.

Primary Care Practitioner Types

Physician (MD or DO)

A Doctor of Medicine (MD) or Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (DO) completes 4 years of undergraduate education, 4 years of medical school, and a 3-year primary care residency (longer for subspecialties). Both must pass national licensing examinations (USMLE for MDs, COMLEX for DOs) and maintain board certification through the American Board of Family Medicine, American Board of Internal Medicine, American Board of Pediatrics, or American Osteopathic Boards. Total training: roughly 11 years post-high-school for general primary care, 12+ for those with a fellowship (e.g., geriatrics).2

Both MDs and DOs have full, independent practice authority in North Carolina and nationally. The clinical training is functionally equivalent; DOs receive additional training in osteopathic manipulative medicine (OMM) — a hands-on technique used primarily for musculoskeletal pain. Most DOs in NC primary care practice essentially the same medicine as MDs; ask your specific DO whether they use OMM in routine visits if that's relevant to your care needs. For insurance purposes there is no distinction — both bill under the same physician fee schedule.

2,072 active MDs/DOs in the four ACA primary care specialties across the RTP region in 2024.3

Physician Assistant (PA)

A PA completes 4 years of undergraduate education plus a 24- to 27-month master's-level PA program at an ARC-PA accredited institution, then passes the Physician Assistant National Certifying Exam (PANCE) through the NCCPA.4 Total training: roughly 6–7 years post-high-school. PA programs require prior clinical experience for admission, and the curriculum mirrors medical school in compressed form — coursework in pharmacology, pathophysiology, and rotations in surgery, internal medicine, pediatrics, and family medicine.

Material change in NC, effective July 1, 2025: Under the Healthcare Workforce Reforms law (Session Law 2025-37 / HB 67),5 experienced PAs — those with more than 4,000 hours of general clinical experience plus 1,000 hours in their chosen specialty — can now practice in team-based settings without a designated supervising physician. Less-experienced PAs and PAs in pain-management practices still require formal physician supervision and monthly quality-improvement meetings for the first six months.

In practice, an experienced PA can serve as your designated PCP, write prescriptions (including most controlled substances), order labs and imaging, and bill independently. PA visits are typically reimbursed at 85% of the physician fee schedule under Medicare; commercial plans often pay similarly. Out-of-pocket costs for the patient are usually identical to a physician visit under most ACA plans.

647 active PAs in the four ACA primary care specialties across the RTP region in 2024.3

Nurse Practitioner (NP)

An NP holds a Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN), an active RN license, and a Master of Science in Nursing (MSN) or Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP) with a population-focused specialty (e.g., Family NP, Adult-Gerontology Primary Care NP, Pediatric NP). Total training: 6–8 years post-high-school. NPs are nationally certified through the American Nurses Credentialing Center or the American Academy of Nurse Practitioners Certification Board, and licensed by the North Carolina Board of Nursing.6

Important NC distinction: North Carolina is classified by the American Association of Nurse Practitioners as a "restricted practice" state. Every NP providing care in NC must hold a written Collaborative Practice Agreement (CPA) with a supervising physician that defines their scope, prescribing authority, and oversight protocols. Within their CPA, NPs have full prescriptive authority including controlled substances. The proposed SAVE Act, which would grant NPs full independent practice authority, has been introduced in multiple sessions but has not passed as of 2026.6

For insurance: NPs are in-network PCP options on virtually all ACA Marketplace plans in NC and are reimbursed at 85% of the physician fee schedule by Medicare. The patient experience is generally indistinguishable from a PA or physician visit at the same practice.

3,488 active NPs (all specialties combined) in the RTP region in 2024 — the largest single category of primary care clinicians.3

Clinical Nurse Specialist (CNS)

A CNS holds an MSN or DNP with specialty preparation in a defined population (acute care, oncology, pediatrics, etc.) and certification through the ANCC. The ACA recognizes CNSs as primary care practitioners, but in practice CNSs in North Carolina overwhelmingly work in hospital and specialty settings rather than as community-based PCPs. You are unlikely to see a CNS listed as your in-network PCP option on an ACA Marketplace plan.

What This Means for Your Insurance Selection

When evaluating a plan's PCP network, the credential mix matters in three concrete ways:

Network depth. In NC's restricted-practice environment, plans rely heavily on NPs to meet network adequacy standards. A "wide network" plan may have many PAs and NPs but fewer physicians accepting new patients. If you specifically want a physician as your PCP, verify acceptance status before choosing.

Continuity. Physician PCPs are more likely to remain in the same practice for 5+ years. PA and NP roles often turn over more frequently; if continuity matters to you (chronic conditions, complex history), this is worth weighing.

Cost equivalence. For a routine ACA-mandated preventive visit (the "free annual checkup"), all four practitioner types deliver the same covered services at the same out-of-pocket cost ($0) when in-network. The credential difference matters most when problems are discovered — a physician's diagnostic depth and ability to order without consultation can shorten the path to specialty care.
