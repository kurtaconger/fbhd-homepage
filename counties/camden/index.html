Health Insurance in Camden County, NC for 2027: All 20 Blue Cross Plans Explained

By Kurt Conger, independent licensed NC health insurance agent (NPN 21720013) · Updated November 5, 2026 · 12 minute read · Plan data: HealthCare.gov Marketplace API, 2027 plan year

If you buy your own health insurance in Camden County (Camden, South Mills and Shiloh) you have one insurer, Blue Cross and Blue Shield of North Carolina, and 20 Marketplace plans to choose from. This guide uses the official HealthCare.gov plan data for 2027 to explain what the plans cost, how they differ, and which one fits which situation.

Key takeaways

  • 20 plans, but only 10 designs. Each design is sold twice: as a Blue Care HMO (statewide network) and a Blue Advantage PPO (nationwide network). Deductibles, copays and out-of-pocket maximums are identical within each pair; the PPO costs about 5.5% more.
  • Full-price premiums for a 45-year-old run $817 to $879 for Bronze, $1,105 to $1,129 for Silver and $1,137 to $1,164 for Gold. A 60-year-old pays about 1.9 times that; a 21-year-old about 0.7 times.
  • Subsidies change everything below about 400% of the poverty level ($63,840 for one person, $86,560 for a couple, $132,000 for a family of four). A 45-year-old earning $30,000 gets every Bronze plan for $0 and a cost-sharing-reduced Silver plan for about $145 a month. Above the line you pay full price.
  • Nobody needs a referral to see a specialist on either network, and emergency care is covered anywhere.
  • The two drug lists are the same list. What changes your medication cost is the plan design, especially the three Standard plans with flat brand-drug copays.
  • No catastrophic plan is offered in Camden for 2027.

The Camden County market at a glance

Camden is a one-carrier county. Blue Cross NC offers four Bronze, three Silver and three Gold designs, and every design comes with a choice of network. That makes the decision simpler than it looks: instead of comparing 20 plans, you make three decisions in order: network, how much coverage, and how your prescriptions are priced. The 10 plan designs are then described one by one, and a situations table maps common Camden households to the plans worth a close look.

What subsidies do to those numbers

The premium tax credit is a fixed dollar amount based on your income and the cost of the second-cheapest Silver plan (here, Blue Care Silver Standard). You can spend it on any plan. Because the enhanced credits of 2021–2025 have expired, the credit stops entirely at 400% of the federal poverty level, and the Marketplace now recovers any overpaid credit in full at tax time, so your income estimate matters. Two things to notice. First, for most subsidized households the cheapest Bronze plans are free or nearly free, which is why the question for many Camden families is not "Bronze or Silver?" but "is a $0 Bronze plan with a $7,000 deductible really cheaper than a $154 Silver plan with an $800 deductible?" (Usually not, if you expect to use care; see Bronze vs. Silver when you take medications.) Second, a single adult under about $22,000 or a couple under about $29,900 is sent to NC Medicaid instead of receiving a Marketplace credit; the application sorts this out.

Decision 1: Blue Care (HMO) or Blue Advantage (PPO)?

This is the easiest decision to get right, because the benefits are identical. Pick a design, and the only differences between its Blue Care and Blue Advantage versions are the network and the price.

  • Blue Care is Blue Cross NC's statewide network. It covers doctors and hospitals across North Carolina, with no referrals required. Out-of-network care is not covered except in an emergency (emergency room visits are covered anywhere in the country at the in-network rate).
  • Blue Advantage uses Blue Cross NC's largest network and adds the national BlueCard network, so you are in-network at Blue Cross doctors in every state. It also has an out-of-network benefit, but a thin one: 50% to 80% coinsurance after the deductible, with no out-of-network out-of-pocket maximum. Treat it as a nationwide in-network plan, not as a plan that pays well for out-of-network care.
  • The price difference is about 5.5% across the board: $44 to $63 a month at age 45, $82 to $119 at age 60. If your subsidy already covers a Bronze plan with money left over, the PPO upgrade can cost you only a few dollars.

Choose Blue Advantage if you spend months outside North Carolina (snowbirds, a second home), have a dependent at an out-of-state college, run a business that keeps you on the road, regularly use a hospital across the Virginia line, or want a specific out-of-state hospital such as Sentara Norfolk, MUSC, Mayo or Johns Hopkins in network. Choose Blue Care if you live here year-round and your doctors are in North Carolina; you get the same benefits for less.

Before enrolling, confirm your doctors in the official directories: Blue Care provider search and Blue Advantage provider search. In Camden County, Sentara Albemarle Medical Center in Elizabeth City is the nearest hospital; many residents also use hospitals in Chesapeake and Norfolk, Virginia, which is where the network choice matters; check the one you would go to.

Decision 2: How much coverage do you expect to use?

Bronze, Silver and Gold describe how costs are split between you and the plan: roughly 60%, 70% and 80% paid by the plan for an average person. In Camden the practical differences are these:

  • Bronze ($817 to $879 at 45): $7,000–$8,700 deductibles, so you pay for most care yourself until something serious happens. All four Bronze plans are HSA-eligible for 2027 under the 2025 federal tax law, which lets you set aside up to $4,500 (single) or $9,000 (family) pre-tax, plus $1,000 if you are 55 or older. Best for people who are healthy, price-driven, or using the plan mainly as protection against a large bill.
  • Silver ($1,105 to $1,129): the plans most people should look at first if they qualify for a subsidy, because incomes under about $39,900 (single) or $82,500 (family of four) unlock cost-sharing reductions that only apply to Silver plans. At $30,000 a 45-year-old's Silver Preferred deductible drops from $2,700 to $325 and the out-of-pocket maximum from $12,000 to $4,000. Without a cost-sharing reduction, Silver is a middle ground that often loses to either Bronze or Gold on total cost.
  • Gold ($1,137 to $1,164): $0–$2,500 deductibles and flat copays for most care. Costs $258–$347 a month more than Bronze at age 45, which is worth it if you take brand-name or specialty drugs, see specialists regularly, or are planning surgery or a pregnancy.

Decision 3: How are your prescriptions priced?

Blue Cross NC uses two drug lists for these plans: a 4-tier list for the three Standard plans (Bronze Standard, Silver Standard, Gold Standard A) and a 5-tier list for the other seven designs. We checked 48 common drugs, from atorvastatin and metformin to Eliquis, Jardiance, Ozempic, Humira and Dupixent, against both lists: coverage was identical on every one. Wegovy and Zepbound were not covered on either list; Ozempic, Mounjaro and Trulicity were. The real difference is tiering. On the 5-tier plans the advertised copay ("$25 Tier 1 Rx") applies to preferred generics only; other generics sit in Tier 2 at a higher copay. On the Standard plans every generic gets the same copay.

What matters far more than the list is how each design treats brand-name and specialty drugs:

  • On Bronze Basic, Silver Preferred, Silver Choice A and Gold Premier A, brand drugs are subject to the deductible (and specialty drugs to 50% coinsurance after it). Someone on a $650-a-month brand drug pays the full deductible before the plan contributes.
  • On Silver Standard and Gold Standard A, preferred brand drugs are a flat $40 or $30 copay with no deductible, and specialty drugs are $350 (after deductible) or $250 (no deductible).
  • On Gold All Copay A, everything is a copay from day one: $60 preferred brand, $120 non-preferred, $220 specialty.

The arithmetic is in Bronze vs. Silver when you take medications, built on these same plans. The short version: one brand-name prescription is enough to make Silver Standard cheaper than Bronze Basic over a year, and a specialty drug makes Gold the cheapest plan on the shelf.

The 10 plan designs, one by one

Bronze

Bronze Basic $817 / $861Best for: healthy adults, anyone whose subsidy makes it free, HSA savers who want the lowest premium

$7,000 deductible (medical and drugs combined) · $12,000 out-of-pocket max · HSA-eligible

The cheapest plan in the county and the one most subsidized households get for $0. Three primary care visits a year are free, then $95; specialists are $175; preferred generics are $25. Everything else, including brand drugs, imaging, the emergency room and hospital stays, is 50% coinsurance after you meet the $7,000 deductible. You are buying protection against a catastrophe, not day-to-day coverage.

Bronze Standard $839 / $883Best for: people who want Bronze pricing but see a doctor a few times a year and prefer copays to coinsurance

$7,500 deductible · $12,000 out-of-pocket max · HSA-eligible · 4-tier drug list

The federally standardized Bronze design: $50 primary care and $100 specialist copays from day one, $25 generics, and after the deductible flat copays of $50 (preferred brand), $100 (non-preferred) and $500 (specialty). The higher deductible is the trade-off for predictable copays and for the simpler 4-tier drug list where every generic costs the same.

Bronze Complete $850 / $897Best for: Bronze buyers with one or two brand-name prescriptions, or who want a lower deductible for a likely procedure without paying for Silver

$4,000 medical deductible plus a separate $1,000 drug deductible · $12,000 out-of-pocket max · HSA-eligible

The lowest medical deductible among the Bronze plans. Primary care is $60, specialists $120, generics $20. Brand drugs become $75 (preferred) or $150 (non-preferred) copays once you have met the separate $1,000 drug deductible, which is reached quickly on any brand medication. Hospital care is 50% after the $4,000 medical deductible.

Bronze HSA Eligible Plus $879 / $926Best for: disciplined HSA savers and anyone who wants the lowest possible worst-case cost on a Bronze premium

$8,700 deductible equal to the out-of-pocket max · HSA-eligible

A true high-deductible plan: you pay everything except preventive care until you reach $8,700, and then the plan pays 100%. There are no copays to track. The appeal is the hard ceiling: $8,700 is the most you can spend in a year on in-network care, versus $12,000 on the other Bronze plans. Pair it with the maximum HSA contribution and the deductible is partly funded with pre-tax dollars.

Silver

Silver Preferred $1,105 / $1,165Best for: households under about 250% of the poverty level who want the lowest Silver premium and mostly generic prescriptions

$2,700 deductible · $12,000 out-of-pocket max · with cost-sharing reductions at $30,000 income: $325 deductible, $4,000 max

The cheapest Silver and the one that benefits most from cost-sharing reductions. Three free primary care visits then $35, specialists $75, generics $15. The weakness is drugs: every brand and specialty drug is 50% coinsurance after the deductible. For a subsidized household with a $325 deductible that hardly matters; at full price with a brand prescription, Silver Standard is the better Silver.

Silver Standard $1,114 / $1,172Best for: anyone taking a preferred brand-name drug, and subsidized households who want flat copays for doctor visits and prescriptions

$6,400 deductible · $10,300 out-of-pocket max · 4-tier drug list · with cost-sharing reductions at $30,000 income: $800 deductible, $3,600 max

The federally standardized Silver and the county's benchmark plan (your subsidy is calculated from its price). The high deductible applies to hospital care, but most everyday care does not touch it: $40 primary care, $80 specialist, $20 generics and $40 preferred brand drugs are copays from day one. Non-preferred brands ($80) and specialty drugs ($350) apply after the deductible.

Silver Choice A $1,129 / $1,190Best for: people who want a lower medical deductible than Silver Standard and take at most one or two brand drugs

$3,500 medical deductible plus $200 drug deductible · $12,000 out-of-pocket max

A middle path between the two other Silvers: three free primary care visits then $45, specialists $95, generics $15, and brand drugs at $35 (preferred) or $70 (non-preferred) copays after a small $200 drug deductible. Specialty drugs and hospital care are 50% after the deductibles. It costs a little more than Silver Standard and in most cases offers less, unless you value the free primary care visits and the lower medical deductible.

Gold

Gold Premier A $1,137 / $1,199Best for: frequent doctor visits and brand-name (not specialty) prescriptions, at the lowest Gold price

$1,800 medical deductible plus $400 drug deductible · $12,000 out-of-pocket max

The cheapest Gold. Three free primary care visits then $15, specialists $45, generics $10, brand drugs $35 or $60 after the $400 drug deductible. Hospital and emergency care are 30% after the $1,800 deductible, and specialty drugs are 50% after the deductible, which is the catch: this plan is strong for doctor visits and ordinary prescriptions but weak for specialty medications.

Gold Standard A $1,148 / $1,208Best for: anyone on a specialty drug, and people who want the lowest possible out-of-pocket maximum

$2,500 deductible · $8,600 out-of-pocket max · 4-tier drug list

The federally standardized Gold, with the lowest out-of-pocket maximum in the county. Primary care $30, specialists $60, generics $15, preferred brand $30, non-preferred $60 and specialty $250, all as copays with no deductible. The deductible applies only to hospital-level care, which is then 25% coinsurance. For about $11 a month more than Gold Premier A you get a far better specialty-drug benefit and a $3,400 lower worst case.

Gold All Copay A $1,164 / $1,227Best for: people who want to know the price of every visit in advance, and those expecting a hospital stay (a $2,500 copay versus 25–50% coinsurance elsewhere)

$0 deductible · $12,000 out-of-pocket max

No deductible at all. Every service is a fixed copay: $30 primary care, $60 specialist, $10 generic, $60 preferred brand, $120 non-preferred, $220 specialty, $1,500 emergency room and $2,500 per hospital stay. You give up the lower out-of-pocket maximum of Gold Standard A in exchange for never having to meet a deductible, which some people find worth about $15 a month for the predictability alone.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Blue Cross the only insurer in Camden County?

For 2027, Blue Cross and Blue Shield of North Carolina is the only carrier offering individual Marketplace plans in Camden County. The other insurers that sell Marketplace plans elsewhere in North Carolina for 2027 (Ambetter, AmeriHealth Caritas Next, Oscar and UnitedHealthcare) do not include Camden in their service areas. That still leaves 20 plans: 10 designs, each sold with a statewide HMO network (Blue Care) and a nationwide PPO network (Blue Advantage).

Is my local hospital in network?

Blue Care is Blue Cross NC's statewide network and Blue Advantage adds the national BlueCard network. In Camden County, Sentara Albemarle Medical Center in Elizabeth City is the nearest hospital; many residents also use hospitals in Chesapeake and Norfolk, Virginia, which is where the network choice matters. Networks change, so confirm your doctors and hospital in the official directory (links in the network section above) before you enroll, and tell us if a specific doctor matters to you.

Do I need a referral to see a specialist?

No. Neither Blue Care nor Blue Advantage requires a referral from a primary care doctor to see a specialist.

Should I buy the PPO if I travel or spend winters out of state?

That is the main reason to. Blue Advantage costs about 5.5% more than the same Blue Care design and gives you in-network access to Blue Cross doctors nationwide. Its out-of-network benefit is weak (50 to 80% coinsurance after the deductible, with no out-of-pocket cap), so the value is the larger in-network footprint, not out-of-network coverage. If you live in North Carolina year-round and your doctors are here, the HMO delivers the same benefits for less.

Is there a catastrophic plan in Camden County?

No. For 2027 no catastrophic plan is offered in Camden County, even for people under 30. The Bronze plans are the lowest-premium options.

What happens if my income is under about $22,000?

North Carolina expanded Medicaid in December 2023. A single adult with income under roughly 138% of the federal poverty level (about $22,000 for one person, $29,900 for a couple, $45,500 for a family of four in 2027) is directed to NC Medicaid rather than receiving a Marketplace subsidy. The Marketplace application checks this automatically.

Do the two drug lists cover different drugs?

No. We tested 48 common brand and generic drugs against both lists and found no differences in coverage. The three Standard plans use Blue Cross NC's 4-tier list with flat copays; the other seven designs use the 5-tier list, where the lowest copay applies only to Tier 1 preferred generics. What changes your drug costs is the plan design (copay vs. coinsurance, and whether the deductible applies), not which list it uses.

Can I keep my plan if I move to another county?

Moving to a new county is a qualifying life event. The same 20 plans are sold in every county from the Crystal Coast to the Albemarle Sound and the Virginia line (see the list at the end of this guide), so a move within that group usually lets you keep your plan; the premium changes if the new county is in a different rating area. A move elsewhere gives you a 60-day special enrollment period to pick a new plan.

When can I enroll?

Open Enrollment for 2027 coverage runs November 1, 2026 through January 15, 2027. Enroll by December 15 for coverage starting January 1. Outside Open Enrollment you need a qualifying event, such as losing other coverage, moving, marriage or a new baby.
