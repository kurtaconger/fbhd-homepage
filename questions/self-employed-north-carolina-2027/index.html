Health Insurance for the Self-Employed in North Carolina (2027): Subsidies, the 400% Cliff and the Premium Deduction

By Kurt Conger, independent licensed NC health insurance agent (NPN 21720013) · Updated November 5, 2026 · 10 minute read · Plan data: HealthCare.gov Marketplace API, 2027 plan year

If you are a sole proprietor, independent contractor, LLC or S corporation owner in North Carolina, you buy your own health insurance, and in 2027 the price depends as much on how you manage your taxable income as on which plan you choose. This guide explains the three things that determine what you pay: the premium tax credit, the income cliff at 400% of the poverty level that returned in 2026, and the self-employed health insurance deduction that can help keep you under it.

Key takeaways

  • The subsidy is based on household MAGI, not gross receipts and not assets. Business expenses, retirement contributions, HSA contributions and the health insurance deduction all reduce it.
  • The 400% cliff is back. Above $63,840 (single), $86,560 (couple) or $132,000 (family of four) there is no credit at all. One dollar over costs a 40-year-old in Carteret County about $5,500 a year; a 64-year-old about $21,900; a couple in their sixties about $46,000.
  • Four levers move you under the line: a SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k), an HSA (every 2027 Bronze plan qualifies), timing of income and expenses, and the self-employed health insurance deduction itself.
  • Repayment caps are gone. From the 2026 tax year, overpaid credits are repaid in full. Estimate carefully and update HealthCare.gov when income changes.
  • Premiums are deductible above the line (Schedule 1), for you, your spouse and dependents, limited to your net business profit, and excluding months you could have joined an employer plan.

How the premium tax credit works in 2027

The Marketplace expects you to spend a set percentage of your income on the benchmark plan, the second-cheapest Silver plan in your county. The percentage rises with income, from about 2% at the poverty level to just under 10% at 400% of it. The credit is the difference between the benchmark premium and that expected contribution, and you can apply it to any Bronze, Silver or Gold plan.

Because the credit is a fixed dollar amount, it buys more of a cheap plan than an expensive one. In Carteret County, a 45-year-old with $45,000 of income gets a credit of about $763 a month: the benchmark Silver Standard costs $362, the cheapest Bronze $62, and Gold Standard A $397. At $30,000 the credit grows to about $971 and every Bronze plan is $0.

The percentages use the federal poverty level for the year before the coverage year. For 2027 coverage that is the 2026 guideline: $15,960 for one person, $21,640 for two, $27,320 for three, $33,000 for four, plus $5,680 for each additional person.

The 400% cliff, in dollars

From 2021 through 2025 the credit faded out gradually above 400% of the poverty level. That enhancement expired at the end of 2025, and for 2027 the original rule applies: at 400% the credit is substantial, and at 400% plus one dollar it is zero. The cost of crossing the line depends on your age, because premiums do and the credit absorbs the difference. No other provision of the tax code punishes a self-employed person this hard for a good year, and no other provision is this easy to plan around, because MAGI is what counts, and you control several of its inputs.

What counts as income

Marketplace income is modified adjusted gross income: the adjusted gross income on your Form 1040, plus tax-exempt interest, the untaxed part of Social Security, and excluded foreign income. For a self-employed household it is built up like this:

  • Net profit from Schedule C (or your share of partnership or S corporation income, plus S corporation wages). Gross receipts minus ordinary business expenses, depreciation and Section 179 deductions.
  • Minus half of your self-employment tax.
  • Minus contributions to a SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA or Solo 401(k).
  • Minus HSA contributions.
  • Minus the self-employed health insurance deduction.
  • Plus everything else on the return: a spouse's W-2 wages, interest, dividends, capital gains, rental income, retirement distributions.

Two things that do not count: money you leave in the business bank account is still income if it was earned, and money you have in savings is not income no matter how much there is. The Marketplace has no asset test.

Four levers that keep you under the line

1. A SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k)

The biggest lever. A SEP-IRA lets you contribute up to 20% of net self-employment earnings (25% of W-2 wages for an S corporation owner), to a limit of $70,000 or more in 2027; a Solo 401(k) adds an employee deferral of $23,500 or more on top. Contributions reduce MAGI dollar for dollar and can be made up to your tax filing deadline, which means you can decide in March how much income to report for the year that just ended.

Worked example: a 40-year-old Carteret consultant nets $75,000. With no retirement contribution she is over 400% and pays $996 a month for Silver Standard, $11,950 a year. A $12,000 SEP contribution brings MAGI to $63,000, the net premium falls to $537, and she keeps $5,500 of credit on top of the income tax saved by the contribution. The $12,000 is still hers.

2. An HSA, and in 2027 every Bronze plan qualifies

Under the 2025 federal tax law, all Bronze and catastrophic Marketplace plans are treated as high-deductible health plans starting in 2026, whatever their copay structure. In 2027 that unlocks a pre-tax HSA contribution of $4,500 (self-only) or $9,000 (family), plus $1,000 if you are 55 or older, deductible above the line and so subtracted from MAGI. HSA money pays your deductible and copays tax-free and rolls over forever. For a self-employed family near the line, a $9,000 HSA contribution and a Bronze plan is often the cleanest combination: lowest premium, biggest deduction, and the deductible partly pre-funded.

3. Timing

Self-employed income is lumpy and partly controllable. Billing a December invoice in January, buying equipment before year-end and expensing it under Section 179, or prepaying deductible expenses all shift MAGI between years. The goal is not to earn less; it is to land each year on the right side of the line, and a year that is going to be over anyway is the year to pull income forward.

4. The self-employed health insurance deduction

Premiums you pay for yourself, your spouse, dependents and children under 27 are deductible on Schedule 1 of your 1040, above the line, even if you do not itemize. Dental and long-term care premiums count too. The deduction is limited to the net profit of the business under which you claim it, and you cannot take it for any month you were eligible for an employer-subsidized plan (yours or your spouse's). If you receive a premium tax credit, you deduct only what you paid after the credit; IRS Publication 974 walks through the circular calculation, and tax software handles it.

The deduction is modest compared with the first two levers (12 months at $537 is $6,444 off MAGI), but it compounds: lower MAGI, bigger credit, and the bigger credit is tax-free.

Repayment caps are gone. Through 2025, if you underestimated income the amount you had to repay at tax time was capped (at $1,500 to $3,500 for most households). Beginning with the 2026 tax year the cap is eliminated. Underestimate by enough to cross 400% and you repay the entire year's credit. Estimate conservatively, report changes on HealthCare.gov as they happen, and treat the four levers as the way to make your estimate come true, not as an afterthought in April.

Choosing the plan once the income question is settled

  • Under 250% of the poverty level (for example a single person under $39,900): start with a Silver plan. Cost-sharing reductions cut the deductible to a few hundred dollars and only apply to Silver. A free Bronze plan is usually the wrong choice unless you take no medications and rarely see a doctor; our Bronze vs. Silver guide shows why.
  • Between 250% and 400%: the credit still helps, but the cost-sharing reduction is gone, and the choice is driven by your expected use. Bronze with a funded HSA for the healthy; Silver Standard or Gold Standard A if anyone takes a brand-name drug.
  • Over 400%, and you cannot get under it: you pay full price, and two more options open up. Off-exchange plans sold directly by insurers through licensed agents include some Blue Cross NC designs not listed on HealthCare.gov, at the same or lower premiums. And if you have at least one non-spouse employee, a small-group plan may beat the individual market: premiums are a business expense and there is no cliff.
  • Whichever band you are in, the network decision is separate: Blue Care (statewide) versus Blue Advantage (nationwide) in most of eastern North Carolina, with Aetna, Ambetter, AmeriHealth Caritas, Cigna, Oscar, UnitedHealthcare and WellCare adding choices in the larger counties.

A checklist for the self-employed before December 15

  1. Estimate 2027 net profit honestly, then subtract planned SEP/401(k) and HSA contributions and half of self-employment tax. That is the income to enter on HealthCare.gov.
  2. Find your household's 400% line in the table above. If your estimate is within $10,000 of it, decide now which lever will keep you under.
  3. Confirm you are not eligible for a spouse's employer plan; if you are, the credit and the deduction are both off the table.
  4. Pick the plan on total cost, not premium, using your actual medications.
  5. Set a calendar reminder to revisit the income estimate in July and October.

Frequently asked questions

Does my business income count for the subsidy, or my personal income?

Your household's modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) on your federal return. For a sole proprietor or single-member LLC that is net profit after business expenses (Schedule C), less half of self-employment tax, retirement contributions, HSA contributions and the self-employed health insurance deduction, plus your spouse's income and any investment income. Gross receipts do not count; a $150,000 business with $90,000 of expenses is $60,000 of income.

What happens if I earn more than I estimated?

You repay the excess credit when you file your taxes, and starting with the 2026 tax year there is no cap on the repayment. If a good year pushes you over 400% of the poverty level, the entire credit for the year is repaid. Report income changes on HealthCare.gov during the year so the credit adjusts, and keep a reserve if your income is unpredictable.

Can I deduct my health insurance premiums if I get a subsidy?

Yes, but only the part you actually paid. If the plan costs $1,125 a month and the credit pays $763, you deduct $362 a month. IRS Publication 974 has the worksheet, and tax software handles the circular calculation (the deduction lowers MAGI, which raises the credit, which lowers the deduction).

I'm an S corporation owner. Does the deduction work the same way?

Nearly. The S corporation pays or reimburses the premium, includes it in your W-2 wages (box 1, not subject to FICA), and you take the self-employed health insurance deduction on your personal return. The policy can be in your name or the company's. Your accountant needs to set this up before year-end W-2s are issued.

My spouse has a job with insurance. Can I still get a subsidy?

Only if the employer's plan is unaffordable or you are not eligible for it. If you could enroll in your spouse's employer plan and the family coverage costs less than about 9% of household income, you are not eligible for a premium tax credit, and you also cannot take the self-employed deduction for months you were eligible for that plan.

Is a small-group plan better once I have employees?

Often. With at least one W-2 employee who is not your spouse, your business can buy a small-group plan. Premiums are a deductible business expense, there is no 400% cliff, and group plans sometimes include network options not sold to individuals. For a one-person business the individual market is usually the better deal because of the subsidy.

What if my income is low this year?

North Carolina expanded Medicaid. If household MAGI is under about 138% of the poverty level ($22,000 for one person, $29,900 for two, $45,500 for a family of four), the Marketplace routes you to NC Medicaid, which has no premium. There is no asset test for this category of Medicaid, so a start-up year with little profit can qualify even if you have savings.
