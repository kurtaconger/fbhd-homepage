Bronze vs. Silver When You Take Medications: Estimated Total Cost, Not Just Premium

By Kurt Conger, independent licensed NC health insurance agent (NPN 21720013) · Updated November 5, 2026 · 9 minute read · Plan data: HealthCare.gov Marketplace API, 2027 plan year

The cheapest premium is rarely the cheapest plan once you fill a prescription. This guide adds up what a year actually costs (premiums plus deductibles, copays and coinsurance) for three kinds of medication users, using the real 2027 Blue Cross NC plans sold in Carteret County. Blue Cross sells these same ten designs in every North Carolina county, so the drug-cost math below is the same wherever you buy Blue Cross and only the premiums change. In the 71 counties where other insurers also sell plans, their designs differ, but the method works on any plan.

Key takeaways

  • Generics only: Bronze wins. A $7,000 deductible never comes into play when your drugs are $15–$25 copays.
  • One brand-name drug: the picture flips. A single preferred brand prescription (Eliquis, Jardiance, Xarelto) costs about $7,400 a year on Bronze Basic but $480 on Silver Standard, enough to make Silver Standard about $3,400 cheaper over the year despite a $300 higher monthly premium.
  • A specialty drug: Gold is cheapest. On Gold All Copay A or Gold Standard A a $3,900-a-month biologic is a $220–$250 copay; on most other plans you hit the out-of-pocket maximum by spring.
  • If you qualify for a subsidy, compare the cheapest Bronze plan (for a 45-year-old at $30,000: free in 37 counties, $20–$74 a month in the other 63) to the $154 benchmark Silver plan with cost-sharing reductions. On Blue Cross Silver the deductible drops to $325–$800 and the worst case to $3,600–$4,000, which usually beats a $7,000 deductible.
  • The plan design matters more than the drug list. Blue Cross NC's two drug lists cover the same drugs; what differs is whether your brand drug is a flat copay or coinsurance after the deductible.

Why the premium is the wrong number to compare

Your yearly cost has two parts: 12 months of premium, which you know in advance, and what you pay when you use the plan, which depends on the plan's rules. For medications the rule that matters is simple to state: does the deductible apply to this drug?

  • If the drug is a copay, you pay the copay every fill from the first one. Silver Standard charges $40 for a preferred brand drug whether it costs $200 or $2,000.
  • If the drug is coinsurance after the deductible, you pay its full negotiated price until you have spent the deductible, then a percentage. On Bronze Basic that is $7,000 first, then 50%.

Brand-name drugs cost $500 to $5,000 a month, so a plan that makes you pay the deductible first turns one prescription into most of a year's out-of-pocket maximum. Generic drugs are $10–$25 copays on nearly every plan, so for generics the deductible never matters.

If you get a subsidy: free Bronze vs. $150 Silver

Most people who buy a Marketplace plan qualify for a premium tax credit, and for them the comparison is different and usually easier. Take the same 45-year-old in Carteret with $30,000 of income. Bronze Basic costs $0 a month. Silver Standard costs $154 a month, and because this income is under 250% of the poverty level the plan comes with cost-sharing reductions: the deductible falls from $6,400 to $800 and the out-of-pocket maximum from $10,300 to $3,600.

For anyone on a brand or specialty drug, the "free" plan is the expensive one. Only a generics-only household should take the free Bronze, and even then a Silver plan with an $800 deductible is cheap insurance against a surprise. One more subtlety: cost-sharing reductions only apply to Silver plans, so a subsidized Gold plan (Gold Standard A at $189 a month for this person) keeps its full $2,500 deductible and is rarely the best buy below 250% of the poverty level.

How this plays out in your county

The $154 is not a Carteret number. At $30,000 this person pays about $154 a month for the benchmark Silver plan in every North Carolina county, because the credit is set so the benchmark costs the same share of income everywhere. What changes is which plan is the benchmark and whether Bronze is truly free. In Carteret and 36 other counties, mostly in the east, the credit is larger than the cheapest Bronze premium, so Bronze is $0. In the other 63, including Mecklenburg, Wake, Guilford, Forsyth, Buncombe and New Hanover, an Ambetter Silver plan sets the benchmark, the cheapest Bronze costs $20 to $74 a month, and Blue Cross Silver Standard costs $228 to $598. Cost-sharing reductions apply to every Silver plan but cut deductibles by different amounts: Ambetter's Clear Silver with $0 Insulin Options, the benchmark in Wake, Buncombe and New Hanover counties, drops to a $2,450 deductible, against $800 on Blue Cross Silver Standard. The comparison is the same everywhere, the cheapest Bronze against the benchmark Silver with cost-sharing reductions, and your county guide lists each plan's price and reduced deductible at this income.

A quick method for your own situation

  1. List your prescriptions and look up each one's tier on the plan's drug list (or let our tool do it).
  2. For each brand or specialty drug, note whether the plan charges a copay or coinsurance after the deductible. If it is after the deductible, assume you will pay the deductible in full.
  3. Add 12 months of premium to the drug costs and your usual visits. Cap the non-premium part at the out-of-pocket maximum.
  4. Do it for two or three candidate plans, not one. The winner is usually obvious once the numbers are side by side.

Frequently asked questions

Is a $0 Bronze plan ever a bad deal?

Yes, whenever you expect to use care. The subsidy that makes Bronze free is the same dollar amount you could put toward Silver, and below 250% of the poverty level Silver plans also come with cost-sharing reductions that cut the deductible to a few hundred dollars. If you take any brand-name drug or see a doctor more than a couple of times a year, a $150-a-month Silver plan whose deductible has been cut to $800 (Blue Cross Silver Standard; other insurers' reductions differ) usually costs less over the year than a free Bronze with a $7,000 deductible.

How do I find out what tier my drug is on?

Every Marketplace plan publishes its drug list (formulary). Our Covanu tool checks your prescriptions against every plan's drug list and estimates what each drug will cost you on each plan, including whether the deductible applies.

What is the difference between a copay and coinsurance for drugs?

A copay is a fixed amount ($40 for a preferred brand drug, say) regardless of the drug's price. Coinsurance is a percentage of the price (50% of a $650 drug is $325). On most Bronze plans and some Silver and Gold plans, brand-name drugs are coinsurance after the deductible, which means you pay the full price until the deductible is met. Plans labeled Standard, and the Gold All Copay plan, use copays instead.

Does the deductible apply to generic drugs?

Rarely, and some generics are free. On every 2027 Blue Cross NC Marketplace plan except Bronze HSA Eligible Plus, generic drugs are a flat copay ($10 to $25) from the first fill. On top of that, the ACA's preventive-care rule makes certain drugs $0 on every plan, including Bronze HSA Eligible Plus, with no deductible: statins for adults 40 to 75 with heart-disease risk factors, low-dose aspirin where recommended, contraceptives, tobacco-cessation products, folic acid in pregnancy and PrEP, among others. Blue Cross NC flags these as preventive generics on its drug list, and our Covanu tool checks your medications against that list. The deductible question is really about brand-name and specialty drugs.

Are GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic and Wegovy covered?

On the 2027 Blue Cross NC Marketplace drug lists we checked, Ozempic, Mounjaro, Trulicity and Rybelsus are covered (for diabetes; prior authorization usually applies). Wegovy and Zepbound, the weight-loss versions, are not covered. Coverage can change during the year, so confirm on the plan's drug list.

What if my drug is not covered at all?

You can ask the insurer for a formulary exception with your doctor's support, use a manufacturer coupon or patient-assistance program, or compare cash prices at discount pharmacies. Tell us the drug before you enroll; the right plan sometimes depends on it.
